Musical Signals

← → November 3, 2026 music, functional programming

The last blog post served as an overview of my music project — what it is, why it is, and roughly what success will look like. Today we’ll get into some of the nitty gritty details that will help put the project onto a firm footing.

In order to have any chance of not confusing ourselves, we must first be precise about what exactly it is we’re talking about. That means we’ll have to model out some types, and, importantly what those types mean. Recall that one of our desiderata is that whatever it is we’re building must be capable of expressing any music from any culture from any time period, including those times and cultures which do not yet exist.

The trick then is to make a series of extensible types, each one providing some aspect of functionality, and letting people compose them together for themselves. Future users can add their own types with their own functionality, and reuse as much of the system that is meaningful to reuse.

By my reckoning, music is made up of some combination of the following:

  1. some notion of time, generally considered to be rhythmic content, or “horizontal structures.”
  2. some notion of simultaneity, generally considered to be chords, polyphony, or “vertical structures.”
  3. things that can happen, generally considered to be musical content of some sort. Perhaps this is pitches (as in musical scores), or waveforms (as in musical playback.)

As a placeholder to get our bearings, let’s imagine the first two elements as types:

type Horizontal a
type Vertical a

and postulate both a Pitch and Waveform type for item three:

type Pitch
type Waveform

By composing these types in different ways, different ideas of music fall out. Several examples:

  1. Horizontal () describes purely time-based structures; eg. rhythmic ideas
  2. Horizontal Pitch is a simple tune, the sort you can whistle
  3. Vertical Pitch is a single chord.
  4. Horizontal (Vertical Pitch) is what you get at a campfire from someone who only knows how to play chords on the guitar. In the jargon, this is “polyphonic” music.
  5. Vertical (Horizontal Pitch) is multiple independent tunes happening simultaneously. In the jargon, this is “contrapuntal” music.
  6. Vertical (Horizontal Waveform) is what you get in a DAW — independent tracks, each of which lets you move recorded samples along its own timeline.

Of course, this is all vibes right now, since we haven’t discussed what exactly any of these types actually are. But this should be enough to wet your whistle; if we can deliver types that match these vibes, we clearly have an amazing starting point.

As a point of note, this first step is where I went wrong in my last public attempt. There I postulated that all music is what here would be described as Vertical (Horizontal a). And I gave unsatisfying answers for what Vertical and Horizontal are, to boot!

Shame. Sorrow.

Anyway. Let’s leave Vertical alone for now, and focus our attention on Horizontal, since it seems to be the crux of the interesting stuff here. However, rather than using up the musically-relevant name Horizontal, let’s go with Signal, since we’re going to be building up this idea in several pieces.

By our vibes, a Signal is a time-based structure. Which is to say, it ought to describe two things: 1) what happens, and 2) when it happens. That’s a pretty big constraint on the search space; the only other question is whether it ought to be continuous or discrete. In the continuous case, we have:

μ (Signal a) = Time -> a

and in the discrete:

μ (Signal a) = [(Time, a)]  -- ordered by monotonically ascending Time

where μ should be read as “the meaning of.” That is to say, we’re trying to pin down the semantics of Signal without saying anything about how we’re actually going to implement this thing.

This is my first point of confusion. Composition (in the programming sense) is significantly better with the Time -> a denotation, but when I listen to music I “hear” it “discretely.” I think. Maybe. Like I said, I am confused.

Rather than choose between these two options, I am cheating and choosing BOTH. HA HA HA. This is intellectually dishonest, but it’s a decision that needs to be made early on, and making progress seems to be dependent on choosing something. Hopefully when everything has clarified down the road, we can return to this decision and make a better choice.

Sorry, let me back up. What the hell am I talking about? I have the advantage of knowing where I’m going, so I imagine it’s pretty disjointed without that. Concretely, I am torn between a few things:

  1. The discrete choice vibes better with my classical piano training, where “music” is rendered as a score with discrete symbols on a page
  2. I have a strong bias towards being able to generate these symbols on a page with this system I’m making
  3. But the discrete choice doesn’t admit nearly as much algebraic structure. For example, it seems very desirable to have an applicative structure on Signal. The usual list applicative is seems musically meaningless (taking the cartesian product of all the notes in a piece? yeah ok). What seems more likely is a pointwise “zippy” applicative. But in a discrete framing, there isn’t enough data to combine two Signal structures that are out of phase with one another.

So, the unsatisfying solution here is to just do both. Rather than picking between a discrete and a continuous representation, just keep both around:

μ (Signal a) = (Time -> a, Set Time)

That is, represent the Signal structure as a continuous function out of time, but also keep track of a set of interesting times!

We get quite a lot of nice algebraic structure on top of this thing:

μ (Signal a) = (Time -> a, Set Time)

instance Functor Signal where
  μ (fmap f a) = first (fmap f) (μ a)

instance Applicative Signal where
  μ (pure a) = (const a, mempty)
  μ (liftA2 f a b) = (liftA2 f *** (<>)) (μ a) (μ b)

instance Semigroup a => Semigroup (Signal a) where
  μ (a <> b) = μ a <> μ b

instance Monoid a => Monoid (Signal a) where
  μ mempty = mempty

instance Foldable Signal where
  μ (foldMap f a) = uncurry foldMap (μ a)

which I always take as a sign that I’m on the right track.

Armed with Signal, we can give some rudimentary combinators on it. For example, we can lift functions:

lift :: (Time -> a) -> Signal a
μ (lift f) = (f, mempty)

and we can generate stepwise signals:

stepwise :: a -> [(Time, a)] -> Signal a
μ (stepwise a0 []) = pure a0
μ (stepwise a0 ((t0, a) : as)) = \t ->
  case t < t0 of
    True -> a0
    False -> μ (stepwise a as)

Attentive readers will notice that nothing yet ever actually introduces any times-of-interest into a Signal. Here is where my earlier belly-aching comes back to bite. I’d LIKE VERY MUCH to be able to say “discrete Signals have a different type than continuous ones.” So maybe we add an Event type:

μ (Event a) = Maybe a

and then say we can make discrete signals:

discrete :: [(Time, a)] -> Signal (Event a)
μ (discrete as) = (\t -> lookup t as, Set.fromList $ fmap fst as)

with the implicit promise that if you have a Signal (Event a) then it must be a discrete Signal! Unfortunately, this is not the sort of promise we can keep; it’s trivial to construct a densely packed Signal of Events:

notActuallyDiscrete :: Signal a -> Signal (Event a)
μ notActuallyDiscrete = fmap Just . μ

Alas. Shame. Sorrow.

Nevertheless, we are just going to ignore this issue, and pretend like any Signal (Event a) must be discrete and sparse! I am eminently aware of how disappointed Conal would be by my motivated reasoning here. I’m disappointed in myself! Unfortunately I don’t have a better answer other than to split up the continuous and discrete cases, but that throws away a bunch of the compositionality that I want. I am confident there is a better solution here but damned if I know what it is.1

Anyway, I’m writing this in the darkness at 5am before my family awakens, but I hear stirring so it’s probably time to stop. If you have any lovely ideas about how to solve this discrete/continuous discrepancy, I’d love to hear them.

  1. One titillating idea is to simply not expose any introductory forms for Event. I haven’t explored this much, since it would mean we don’t get an applicative functor over Event. Thinking about it now, it’d still be fine to expose the Nothing-analogous constructor, so we could still give mempty. And perhaps this is the first motivating case I’ve ever found for giving only a Apply instance instead of a full Applicative.↩︎
